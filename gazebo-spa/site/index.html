The Garden Bathhouse

Five plans for a Korean-style bathhouse on the land: one hot tub under a roof with plants inside, a cedar sauna a few steps away, then cold water and a warm floor to rest on. The starting point is the enclosed pool house at The Spring in Desert Hot Springs.

Every image is a Blender render of the real lot model: same slope, same Joshua trees, same mountains to the north. Prices are 2025-2026 list prices or labeled estimates.

The precedent

What The Spring's gazebo really is

The "gazebo" at The Spring Resort & Spa is an enclosed pool house at one end of the main pool, which one guide calls "the pool inside the greenhouse". The resort keeps that pool "slightly above body temperature" (a hot-springs guide puts it at about 101F) and the outdoor soaking pool at 104F. The property was built in 1957, was known for more than 45 years as The Moors Hotel, became The Spring in 2003, and is adults-only. Reconstructed from the resort's own photos, the pool house is:

  • A one-story timber post-and-beam room under a low-slope roof with exposed rafter tails. Posts are roughly 6 ft apart, about 8 ft to the top plate.
  • Walls in three bands: a solid knee wall about 3 ft high, glass with a frosted privacy stripe, and an open band of painted vertical slats under the header.
  • A dark-bronze sliding glass door to the main pool.
  • Inside: a freeform pool about 4 ft deep with a blue mosaic band at the waterline, a pebble-finish deck, wood sling chairs, boulders, potted yucca and snake plant, and fan palms just outside.
  • Around it: pergolas of rough-sawn posts with vines and hanging rusted cone planters, fan palms, and black pebble mulch.

What these plans borrow: the glass room that stops the wind, warm water a little above body temperature, plants growing right beside the tub, a slatted band that lets steam out, and a pergola with vines. What they add: the Korean order of wash, soak, sauna, cold and rest; coral lattice in place of frosted glass; and blush, peach and black against white plaster and cedar.

How you would use it

The bathing round

A Korean bathhouse (mogyoktang) and its rest halls (jjimjilbang) follow one order: clean, hot, cold, rest. For two people at home a full round takes about 2 to 2.5 hours. Steps 3 and 4 repeat two or three times.

  1. WashSeated shower on a low stool, about 10 minutes. Hair tied up. Nobody gets in the tub unwashed.
  2. Soak10 to 15 minutes at 100-104F, heart above the water. In summer run it as a warm tub at 97-100F.
  3. Sauna8 to 12 minutes at 176-194F. About 30 minutes of high heat in total.
  4. Cold30 seconds to 2 minutes at 57-64F, head out, feet first, never alone. In winter the water is cold enough on its own.
  5. Rest10 to 15 minutes lying on the warm floor (about 97F) with a glass of water.

Once a week, after a soak, scrub each other with an Italy towel (seshin), then rinse. Finish with cold sikhye (sweet rice drink) and baked eggs on the warm floor.

Safety. Korean bathhouses run their hot tubs at 106-109F; at home, cap the tub at 104F (the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's limit since 1979) and at 100F or less for anyone pregnant. Drink water after every round and skip alcohol before and between rounds. Anyone with heart conditions should ask a doctor about sauna and cold plunges first. Keep the warm floor at or below about 104F for long lying.

Five plans

Five ways to build it

All five sit on the same spot: a level terrace just south-east of the house in land Scheme A, more than 50 ft from every Joshua tree. Each has one hot tub, a cedar sauna a few steps away, a cold plunge or cold shower, a wash and scrub station, a warm rest platform, and plants inside. Click any image to enlarge it.

Spring Glass House

Closest to The Spring Budget tier: Premium

A 24 x 18 ft timber-and-glass bath house, built the way The Spring built its enclosed pool: a solid knee wall, a band of glass, and an open band of slats under a low roof. Where The Spring has a frosted privacy stripe across the glass, this one has a strip of coral lattice at eye level. Sliding glass doors open south to the view and east to a small sauna courtyard.

Size

About 432 sq ft under roof. Terrace 51 x 37 ft.

Best for

Year-round use. Glass and a solid knee wall stop the spring wind and hold heat on 20F nights, and steam leaves through the slats.

Watch out for

The most permits: an enclosed building, safety glass near the water, and an in-ground spa that needs a barrier or safety features. The inspector may treat the room as indoor, which changes the wiring rules. Highest cost. Needs an exhaust fan for steam.

Jeongja Bathhouse

The full Korean bath Budget tier: Balanced to premium

A hanok-style pavilion (jeongja) on a 2-ft stone platform, 24 x 16 ft, in three 8-ft bays that follow the Korean bath in order: wash, soak, rest. Paper-backed lattice screens close the north and west sides for privacy and glow at night. The south side stays open to the view, and the black tile roof turns up at the corners.

Plan key

Under the roof

  • Bay 2: a sunken tiled tub, 6.5 x 6.5 ft, set into the platform
  • Bay 3: a heated ondol rest platform, 7 x 8 ft, with floor cushions and a sikhye and baked-egg station

Around it

  • Cedar cold-plunge tub and a rinse shower

Size

About 384 sq ft under roof. Stone platform 28 x 20 ft.

Best for

Doing the whole ritual in order: wash, soak, sauna, cold, rest. It is the most clearly Korean of the five.

The curved roof is custom carpentry; a straight hip roof costs less. A sunken tub needs a drain and a structural pit. Use polycarbonate behind the paper so rain and splash don't ruin it.

Planting

Plants inside, natives outside

The lot is USDA zone 9a: winter lows of 20-25F, with a record 17F and 115F at the Joshua Tree station. Inside a partly enclosed bathhouse it stays warmer and more humid, but plants still need drip irrigation and a winter plan. Keep plants out of the splash zone; tub chemicals burn leaves.

Before you build

The rules that shape it

  • Joshua trees. Any digging, grading, slab, trench or path within 50 ft of a western Joshua tree (a neighbor's counts too) needs approval from California Fish and Wildlife first. Treat 50 ft as the trigger, not a safe distance: the agency sets buffers project by project. Map the trees before siting anything, including the power and water trenches.
  • When you can build it. In the County, an accessory structure needs a legally established main use; until then it can only be built under a Temporary Use Permit. Nobody has confirmed whether the trailer permit counts as that main use, so ask County Building and Safety before buying a gazebo or tub.
  • Building permits. Needed for any structure over 120 sq ft, any patio cover (solid or lattice) at any size, any pool or spa, and all electrical work. A small portable spa is exempt only from the planning permit, not from these.
  • Setbacks and coverage (if the lot is zoned Rural Living in the unincorporated County): 25 ft front, 15 ft rear, 15 ft sides on lots wider than 75 ft, and no more than 20% of the lot under structures and paving combined. Inside the Town of Yucca Valley the Town's own rules apply instead; they were not checked here.
  • Drowning prevention. State law exempts a hot tub with a locking safety cover labeled ASTM F1346. The County's barrier bulletin still describes a cover plus one more safety feature, so the inspector decides. If asked for a second feature, don't use a pool alarm: state law says a cover plus an alarm doesn't count.
  • Wiring. A 240 V hot tub needs a 50 A GFCI disconnect within sight of the tub, at least 5 ft away (Hot Spring says no more than 50 ft). For an outdoor tub, no light or fan within 5 ft of the water unless it is 12 ft or more above it; a 16 x 16 kit gazebo is only 11 ft tall, so light from the edges. If the inspector treats an enclosed bathhouse like Scheme A as indoor, the rules change. These are 2023 National Electrical Code figures; confirm them with the County or your electrician. A 6 kW sauna heater needs its own 30 A circuit.
  • Wind. The County designs for open terrain (Exposure C, ASCE 7-22). Spring gusts reach 40-50 mph, so anchor any gazebo to engineered footings and strap the tub cover.
  • Water. A 7 x 7 tub holds about 340 gallons and is drained three or four times a year; an uncovered tub can lose over 2,000 gallons a year to evaporation. Keep it covered.

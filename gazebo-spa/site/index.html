The Garden Bathhouse

Five plans for a Korean-style bathhouse on the land: one hot tub built into the ground, under a roof with plants inside, a cedar sauna a few steps away, then cold water and a warm floor to rest on. The starting point is the enclosed pool house at The Spring in Desert Hot Springs.

The precedent

What The Spring's gazebo really is

The "gazebo" at The Spring Resort & Spa is an enclosed pool house at one end of the main pool, which one guide calls "the pool inside the greenhouse". The resort keeps that pool "slightly above body temperature" (a hot-springs guide puts it at about 101F) and the outdoor soaking pool at 104F. The property was built in 1957, was known for more than 45 years as The Moors Hotel, became The Spring in 2003, and is adults-only. Reconstructed from the resort's own photos, the pool house is:

  • A one-story timber post-and-beam room under a low-slope roof with exposed rafter tails. Posts are roughly 6 ft apart, about 8 ft to the top plate.
  • Walls in three bands: a solid knee wall about 3 ft high, glass with a frosted privacy stripe, and an open band of painted vertical slats under the header.
  • A dark-bronze sliding glass door to the main pool.
  • Inside: a freeform pool about 4 ft deep with a blue mosaic band at the waterline, a pebble-finish deck, wood sling chairs, boulders, potted yucca and snake plant, and fan palms just outside.
  • Around it: pergolas of rough-sawn posts with vines and hanging rusted cone planters, fan palms, and black pebble mulch.

What these plans borrow: the glass room that stops the wind, warm water a little above body temperature, plants growing right beside the tub, a slatted band that lets steam out, and a pergola with vines. What they add: the Korean order of wash, soak, sauna, cold and rest; coral lattice in place of frosted glass; and blush, peach and black against white plaster and cedar.

How you would use it

The bathing round

A mogyoktang is a Korean bathhouse for washing and soaking; a jjimjilbang adds hot rooms and a heated floor to lie on. Both follow the same pattern: clean, hot, cold, rest. For two people at home a full round takes about 2 to 2.5 hours. Steps 3, 4 and 5 repeat two or three times, and the round ends with a longer 20-30 minute rest.

  1. WashSeated shower on a low stool, about 10 minutes. Hair tied up. Nobody gets in the tub unwashed.
  2. Soak10 to 15 minutes at 100-104F, heart above the water. In summer run it as a warm tub at 97-100F.
  3. Sauna8 to 12 minutes at 176-194F. No more than about 30 minutes of high heat in total.
  4. Cold30 seconds to 2 minutes at 57-64F, head out, feet first, never alone. From about November to April the water is cold enough on its own.
  5. Rest10 to 15 minutes lying on the warm floor (about 97F) with a glass of water.

Once a week at most, after a soak, scrub each other with an Italy towel, a coarse mitt (the scrub itself is called seshin). Never on the face; rinse after. Finish lying on the warm floor with cold sikhye (a sweet rice drink) and slow-baked eggs (maekbanseok eggs, baked ahead in an oven or rice cooker until the whites turn tan).

Safety. Korean bathhouses run their hot tubs at 106-109F. At home, cap the tub at 104F, the long-standing U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission guideline (from 1979; advice, not a regulation), and at 100F or less for anyone pregnant. Drink water after every round and skip alcohol before and between rounds. Anyone with a heart condition should ask a doctor about sauna and cold plunges first. Keep the warm floor at or below about 104F for long lying.

What to wear. Korean bathhouses are nude and split by gender. At home with guests, wear swimsuits in the tub and loose cotton clothes (jjimjilbok, a T-shirt and shorts) in the sauna and on the warm floor; peach sets for guests suit the palette.

Barrel or cabin sauna? A barrel heats fast and costs less, but you sit upright. A 7 x 7 ft cabin has a bench long enough to lie on, which is how many Koreans rest in the heat. Ask for red cedar: Almost Heaven's barrels come in red cedar, while some other makers use hemlock or spruce.

Which one?

To start soon and for the least money: E, then grow toward C. For the full Korean bath in order: B. For The Spring's glass room and comfort on cold nights: A. If plants and privacy matter most: D.

Decide these first: open or enclosed; how the in-ground tub is built (tiled concrete, fiberglass shell, or a recessed acrylic tub); barrel or cabin sauna; electric or wood-fired heat; how many people will use it at once.

Can we build it ourselves? In every plan, hire the electrician, the engineer for the footings, and a licensed builder for the in-ground tub.

  • E: Yes, in a few weekends once the pit is dug and lined.
  • C: Mostly, from a kit; hire the pool builder for the tub.
  • B: Hire a carpenter for the curved roof and a pool builder for the tub.
  • A: Hire a contractor, a glazier and a pool builder.
  • D: Hire a mason for the walls and a pool builder for the tub.

A way to phase it. Because the tub is built into the ground, pick its final spot from the start. Build the tub where it will stay, roof it simply at first (E), and add the bigger structure of A, B, C or D around it later. The barrel sauna fits C, and the plunge and shower move into any plan. Start only once the County confirms the trailer permit counts as the main use, or issues a Temporary Use Permit.

Every cost here is a planning estimate, not a quote; get two or three local bids. Ranges include digging, the shell, plumbing, a heat pump, bonding and a cover, but not the roof structure, barrier, long trenches or soils engineering. Caliche (hardpan) under the sand can add $1,000-4,000 to the dig.

Heating is a heat pump now. Since January 1, 2026, California's energy code requires the main heater for a new permanent spa to be a heat pump (sized to the code's method) or solar. An electric or propane heater can only be a backup. A listed portable electric spa (Scheme E) is exempt; ask plan check whether a recessed one still counts. Most heat pumps stop heating below about 40-50F, so on 17-25F nights the tub relies on a tight cover, daytime recovery and a backup heater. The cover is required anyway, and standing pilot lights are banned.

A tub this deep counts as a pool. The County treats any water over 18 in deep, hot tubs included, as a pool. That means a 60-in barrier (gates single, no wider than 4 ft, opening outward, self-closing, with the latch 60 in or higher), or a locking safety cover plus one more feature: a door exit alarm, a high door latch, or an in-water alarm. State law exempts spas with locking ASTM F1346 covers, but the County's handout doesn't mention that, so ask in writing and budget for a cover plus one alarm. A cover only counts if its label says F1346-23 (many say F1346-91). Single covers come only up to 96 in, so Scheme A's 9.5 x 7 ft tub needs a two-piece or automatic cover. The cold plunge is a pool too if its water is over 18 in deep.

Bonding. Every in-ground tub needs its steel and the deck within 3 ft bonded together under the paving, and a recessed portable spa loses the exemption a free-standing one has, so its deck needs bonding too. Design the grid before the slab is poured.

Living with it

Summer, water and running costs

June to September: use the bathhouse at dawn or after dark. Set the tub to 97-100F, keep sauna rounds short, and run a chiller on the plunge; without one it is only cold from about November to April. A glass room (Scheme A) overheats on summer afternoons unless its doors open wide and the west and south glass is shaded.

Winter: nights reach 17-25F. Keep the tub covered and heated, and disconnect and drain outdoor plunge chillers on freezing nights (Penguin rates its outdoor units only above 32F). Don't open a cover lifter in winds above about 25 mph.

Where the water goes: decide before pouring a slab. Shower and wash-floor water goes to the septic system or to a permitted graywater line for the plants; ask the County which it allows. To drain the tub, let the chlorine fade for a few days, then drain it slowly onto the land, not into the septic tank. Ask the County before the first drain.

Running costs (rough estimates at about 35 cents per kWh; check your utility): a covered tub about $40-90 a month in winter and less in summer; a sauna session about $2-3; a plunge chiller about $20-50 a month in summer; the warm floor about 50 cents for two hours.

Before you build

The rules that shape it

  • Check the jurisdiction first. Everything here assumes unincorporated San Bernardino County. Confirm by parcel number; inside the Town of Yucca Valley the Town's rules apply instead, and they weren't checked here.
  • Joshua trees. Any digging, grading, slab, trench or path within 50 ft of a western Joshua tree (a neighbor's counts too) needs approval from California Fish and Wildlife first. Treat 50 ft as the trigger, not a safe distance. If a permit is needed, the in-lieu fee is $156.50-2,609 per tree depending on its height and the fee area. Map the trees before siting anything, including the power and water trenches.
  • When you can build it. An accessory structure needs a legally established main use; until then it can only be built under a Temporary Use Permit. Nobody has confirmed whether the trailer permit counts, so ask County Building and Safety before buying anything. Starting work before a permit is issued adds a fee equal to the permit.
  • Permits. The spa permit is $335 plus a $57 intake fee, and $195 more for plan review if it isn't a standard plan (County fee schedule effective July 1, 2026); a large site-built tub may be billed as a pool instead ($704-1,000). The gazebo or bathhouse needs its own building permit (any patio cover, solid or lattice, or anything over 120 sq ft), and all electrical work needs a permit. A grading permit is needed only if all grading on the site together tops 100 cubic yards.
  • Setbacks and coverage (Rural Living zoning): only the tub itself may sit in the rear setback (at least 5 ft from the line); any roof over it must meet the full 25 ft front and 15 ft side and rear setbacks (sides on lots wider than 75 ft). Walls inside a side or rear setback are capped at 6 ft. No more than 20% of the lot may be under structures and paving combined.
  • Septic and well. Keep roofed structures at least 5 ft from the septic tank and 8 ft from the leach field, and the septic system 100 ft from a well. Never drain the tub into the septic system; drain it, once the chlorine has faded, onto an area at least 10 ft from the septic tank and leach field. Ask County Environmental Health before the first drain.
  • Wiring and lights. A hardwired tub needs its disconnect within sight of the tub and at least 5 ft away; Hot Spring's 240 V tubs need a 50 A GFCI subpanel no more than 50 ft away. Outdoors (B to E), a line-voltage light within 5 ft of the water must be 12 ft above it; under a low gazebo roof, use listed low-voltage spa lights or keep fixtures farther away. Indoors (A): 12 ft without GFCI, 7.5 ft with GFCI, and lower only for listed enclosed types with GFCI. A 6 kW sauna heater needs its own 30 A circuit. These follow the 2023 National Electrical Code; confirm with your electrician.
  • Warm floors near the tub. The electrical code restricts electric heating cables in or under the deck within 20 ft of a pool, so heat the rest platforms with hot water (hydronic tubing) rather than electric mats.
  • Wind. The County assumes open, flat land with nothing to block the wind ("Exposure C" in the building code). Spring wind advisories bring gusts of 40-50 mph, so anchor any gazebo to engineered footings and strap the tub cover.
  • Fire. The parcel's fire hazard zone hasn't been checked. If it is mapped, ember-resistant building rules may apply. Prefer noncombustible roofing, keep 5 ft of gravel around structures, use electric heat, and never light a wood-fired stove on windy or red-flag days.

Lean (Scheme E)

about $24,000 - $64,000

  • Acrylic spa recessed into a drained concrete pit with service access, wiring and bonding (estimate), $12,000-40,000
  • 12 x 12 to 12 x 16 ft cedar gazebo kit, $2,599-4,340
  • 2-person cedar barrel sauna (Almost Heaven Salem), $4,485
  • Stock-tank plunge (no chiller) or Ice Barrel, $300-1,750
  • Outdoor shower and bench, warm platform (estimates), $1,600-4,500
  • Sauna circuit, permits, plants, a safety cover and one alarm (estimates), $3,000-9,000

Balanced (Scheme C; B with a simpler roof)

about $53,000 - $123,000

  • Fiberglass in-ground shell with heat pump, bonding and cover (estimate), $15,000-45,000
  • Timber-frame or octagon gazebo kit with coral screens and a west windbreak, $9,100-14,200
  • 2-4 person cedar barrel sauna, $5,184 (installation from $1,400 more) or a 6-person barrel at $7,799
  • Cold plunge with a chiller, $3,950-6,790
  • Tiled warm platform with hot-water floor heat, and a wash station (estimates), $4,500-11,000
  • Slab, spa subpanel, permits, wind engineering, plants, lighting (estimates), $15,000-38,000

Premium (Schemes A and D; B as drawn)

about $128,000 - $316,000
